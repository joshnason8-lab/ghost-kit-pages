# Cultural care

The Mesoamerican ballgame is a living tradition. Ulama is kept alive by a few families and teams in the villages of
southern Sinaloa. Teams in the Yucatán, Quintana Roo, Belize, Guatemala and Honduras have revived pitz / pelota maya.
Pok Ta Pok Rivals tries to show the game as it is played, proudly and plainly. This page lists what the game chose
and what a knowledgeable reviewer should check: a player, a coach, an ethnographer, or a native speaker of Yucatec
Maya. Sources for the rules are in docs/RULES.md.

## What the game does, and does not do

- **Sport and ceremony with respect.** The game shows the sport only. It has no sacrifice imagery and no jokes about
  it, and no "Aztec warrior" or feathered-headdress stereotypes. The "Rivals" of the title are towns facing each
  other on a court, not a fight.
- **Places as plain place names only.** Sixteen towns: eight Sinaloa villages where ulama has been played
  (Escuinapa, Los Llanitos, La Sávila, El Quelite, Villa Unión, El Chilillo, La Mora Escarbada, El Rosario), and
  towns of the Maya region (Pisté, Valladolid, Izamal, Felipe Carrillo Puerto, Palenque, Flores, Orange Walk, Copán
  Ruinas). Each team has a nickname of the game's own: an animal, a tree or a bird of the place (Garzas,
  Huizaches, Coyotes, Chachalacas, Pelícanos, Saraguatos, Toucans, Guacamayas). Two nicknames are Yucatec Maya
  words: *K'iin* (sun) and *Ya'axche'* (ceiba). No real club, federation, tournament or family is named.
- **Courts as plain descriptions.** "Village court, Sinaloa", "Festival court", "Stone court, Yucatán forest",
  "Stone court by torchlight". No archaeological site or tourist park is named or copied. The stone court is
  generic: the size of the smaller Yucatán courts, with the I shape and rings of the later courts.
- **Players invented.** Names are a given name and a family name common in the region (Sinaloa, Yucatec Maya,
  Guatemalan and Belizean families), combined at random. No player's real name is used on purpose. One family
  known to keep ulama alive in Escuinapa is left out of the name lists so it cannot appear by chance.

## The judges' signals

No source describes how the veedores signal. The game's own gestures:

- **A raya:** an arm raised toward the half of the side that wins it.
- **A fault:** arms crossed over the head first, then the arm toward the winning side.

**For a reviewer:** how do the veedores call a raya? Do they call it aloud? What do they say? Do they mark the count
on the ground or on a board? The game's count board shows tally strokes (rayas), as the word suggests.

## Name of the game

"Pok Ta Pok Rivals" is the owner's choice. *Pok-ta-pok* comes from Frans Blom's 1932 article. The OED's editor treats
it as Blom's garbling of the Yucatec *pokolpok*
([languagehat](https://languagehat.com/pok-ta-pok/)). It is nonetheless the name the revival goes by in the Yucatán
today. How to Play's "Words" card says where the name comes from.

## Things a reviewer may still find

- **The crowd's look.** Figures are rendered from the game's own bodies: everyday clothes, straw hats and caps. Does a
  Sinaloa village crowd look like this?
- **The festival court's papel picado.** It is a general Mexican festive touch, not specific to ulama.
- **Music.** The menus are quiet. Music, if added, should come from the region and be credited, never "generic
  ancient".
