# The ballgame

People in Mesoamerica have played with rubber balls for more than three thousand years. Rubber balls preserved at El
Manatí, on Mexico's Gulf Coast, date to around 1600 BCE. Archaeologists know of more than two thousand ball courts,
from Central America to northern Mexico. The Classic Maya called the game *pitz*. In Nahuatl it was
*ōllamaliztli*, from *ōlli*, rubber, and the court was the *tlachtli*.

The game mattered beyond sport. Carvings, murals and the chroniclers of the sixteenth century connect it to ritual,
politics and cosmology. Some carvings link it with sacrifice, but how often, and in what way, is still debated by
scholars. The ancient rules are not fully known, and they surely varied across so many centuries and peoples. So
every modern version, this game's classic court included, is partly a reconstruction.

The game never died out. **Ulama** is still played in villages of southern Sinaloa, Mexico, by a few families and
teams who keep it alive. Two sides strike a solid rubber ball of three to four kilograms with the hip alone,
across the *analco* line in the middle of a long earth court called the *taste*. In recent decades,
teams in the Yucatán, Quintana Roo, Belize, Guatemala and Honduras have also revived the game as **pok-ta-pok** or
**pelota maya**, with their own rules and tournaments.

It is a living tradition, and the people who play it are its keepers.
