## What is kho kho?

Kho kho is a fast tag game from India, played by two teams on a rectangular court with a wooden pole at each end.

One team **chases** and the other **defends**. Most of the chasers sit or squat in a line down the middle of the court, facing alternate ways. Only one chaser is "active" at a time. To pass the chase on, the active chaser touches a sitting teammate on the back and calls out **"kho!"** The two swap roles, and the new chaser sets off at once.

The defenders come on in small groups and try to dodge and survive as long as they can. Because chasers can only run in set directions, the game is a mix of sprinting, dodging and teamwork.

The name comes from the Marathi word *kho-kho*; "kho" imitates the sound players make when passing on the chase.

## History and origins

### Tradition and early forms

Kho kho is one of India's traditional games, but its early history is hard to pin down. Some writers claim it has been played since at least the 4th century BCE, and some link parts of it to episodes in the *Mahabharata*. Wikipedia notes that the early-dating claim needs better sources, so these links are best treated as tradition rather than established history.

Tradition also describes an older version called **Rathera**, said to have been played on chariots (*ratha* means chariot in Sanskrit).

### The modern game

The modern game was organised in **1914**, when the **Deccan Gymkhana** club in Pune gave it formal rules. The first rule book is reportedly linked to the independence leader Bal Gangadhar Tilak.

Kho kho was shown at the **1936 Berlin Olympics** as a demonstration sport, alongside kabaddi. The national body, now the **Kho Kho Federation of India (KKFI)**, was founded in 1955 as the Akhil Bharatiya Kho Kho Mandal, and held its first competition in 1959.

Kho kho was demonstrated at the 1982 Asian Games in Delhi, and became a medal sport at the South Asian Games in 2016. The game was traditionally played on earth; top-level matches now use mats.

## How it's played

There are two main formats today. The **standard format** is the traditional game run by the KKFI at national championships. The **fast format**, with seven chasers on court, is used by the Ultimate Kho Kho league and was used at the 2025 World Cup. Measurements below are for senior players; junior courts are smaller.

### The court

{{diagram}}

Under the standard rules the court is **27 m long and 16 m wide**.

- A wooden **pole** stands at each end, **24 m apart**. Each pole is 120–125 cm high and 9–10 cm thick, smooth and round.
- A **central lane**, 30 cm wide, runs down the middle from pole to pole.
- **Cross lanes**, 35 cm wide, cross the central lane at right angles. The chasers sit where they meet. Neighbouring cross lanes are 2.3 m apart.
- Behind each pole is a **free zone**, about 1.5 m deep, where the active chaser can move freely and change direction.

The central lane splits the court into two halves. The fast format uses a shorter court, **22 m by 16 m**, and chasers squat in marked 40 cm squares.

### Teams

In the standard game a squad has up to 15 players, **12** are named for a match, and **nine** take the field. When chasing, eight sit in the central lane and one is the active chaser. When defending, players come on in **batches of three**. When all three in a batch are out, the next batch must come in straight away.

In the fast format, **seven** chasers are on court.

### Sitting chasers and the "kho"

The sitting chasers face alternate sides of the court: one faces left, the next faces right, and so on. They must not get up or move until they get a kho.

To give a valid kho, the active chaser:

1. comes up behind a sitting teammate,
2. touches them on the back (between the shoulders and the waist),
3. and says "kho" clearly.

The kho must be given before the active chaser passes the cross lane of the player they mean to touch. The new chaser then gets up and runs; the old one sits down in the empty spot.

### Direction rules

These rules are what make kho kho tricky:

- A new chaser may only go into the half of the court they were facing while sitting.
- Once a chaser has set off towards one pole, they must keep going that way. Going back over ground already covered is called **receding** and is a foul.
- The active chaser may not cross the central lane into the other half. To change halves or direction, they must run round a pole through the free zone, or pass the chase on with a kho.

If a chaser breaks a rule, the umpire calls a foul. Until it is cleared, the chasers cannot get anyone out. The chaser clears it by turning away and running the other way until they give a kho or reach a free zone.

### Dives

Diving is one of the most exciting parts of the game:

- In a **pole dive**, the chaser holds a pole with one hand and stretches out to tag a defender with the other.
- In a **sky dive**, the chaser leaps through the air to tag a defender who is just out of reach.

Some direction rules are relaxed during a pole dive, but touching or crossing the central lane is still a foul.

### Scoring

A defender is out, and the chasers score, when:

- the active chaser tags them fairly with a hand,
- they step completely out of the court,
- or they are late coming on with a new batch.

In the standard game each out is worth one point. In the fast format each tag is worth **two points**, and a batch of defenders that survives at least three minutes earns a **Dream Run** bonus point for the defending side, plus a point for each extra 30 seconds.

The fast format also has a **wazir**: one chaser who may change direction. During a **powerplay**, the chasing team can have two wazirs until the current batch of defenders is out.

### Officials

A senior match is run by a **referee**, **two umpires**, a **timekeeper** and a **scorer**, usually with an assistant scorer. The umpires call fouls with the whistle.

### Match length

A standard match has **two innings**. In each innings, each team chases for one **turn** and defends for one turn. A senior turn lasts **9 minutes**, with short breaks between turns and a longer break between innings.

In the fast format each turn is **7 minutes**. If a match is level, a **minimum chase** is used: each team gets one extra turn, and the first to score wins.

## Equipment

Kho kho needs very little:

- Two smooth wooden poles fixed at the ends of the court.
- A marked court, either earth or, at top level, mats.
- Light shoes and a sports kit with numbers.

There is no ball. Wikipedia notes that playing on mats takes more energy than playing on earth and may lead to more injuries.

## Where it's played today

### Governing bodies

- The **Kho Kho Federation of India (KKFI)** runs the sport in India. It holds national championships every year for men, women and juniors.
- The **International Kho Kho Federation (IKKF)** was founded in 2018 and is based in New Delhi. It wants to spread the game to enough countries to be considered for the Olympics.
- The **Asian Kho Kho Federation** runs the Asian Championship. India won the 4th edition in 2023.

### Ultimate Kho Kho

**Ultimate Kho Kho (UKK)** is a professional franchise league run with the KKFI. It started in 2022 with six teams and uses the seven-a-side fast format, with seven-minute turns.

The first season ran from 14 August to 4 September 2022 and was broadcast by Sony. Odisha Juggernauts won the first final 46–45 against Telugu Yoddhas. Gujarat Giants won the 2023–24 season, beating Chennai Quick Guns 31–26.

### The first Kho Kho World Cup (2025)

The first **Kho Kho World Cup** was held at the Indira Gandhi Indoor Stadium in **New Delhi from 13 to 19 January 2025**, organised by the KKFI and the IKKF. It used the fast seven-a-side format.

Reports differ on the number of countries: news reports at the opening said 20, while Wikipedia lists 23 countries, with 20 men's and 19 women's teams.

India won both titles, unbeaten, beating **Nepal in both finals**:

- **Women:** India 78–40 Nepal.
- **Men:** India 54–36 Nepal.

## Famous moments and players

- **Priyanka Ingle.** The Pune player captained India's women to the 2025 World Cup title and was named best player of the tournament. She won the Rani Laxmibai Award in 2022. (Source: Wikipedia.)
- **Chaithra B's Dream Run (2025).** In the last turn of the women's World Cup final, Chaithra B's batch of defenders stayed in for 5 minutes 14 seconds, earning Dream Run points that closed out India's win. (Source: The Bridge; Wikipedia.)
- **Pratik Waikar.** He captained India's men to the 2025 World Cup title. (Source: The Bridge.)
- **Ramji Kashyap.** He was named Player of the Season in both of the first two Ultimate Kho Kho seasons. (Source: Wikipedia, Ultimate Kho Kho.)
- **Odisha Juggernauts' one-point title (2022).** The first Ultimate Kho Kho final was decided 46–45. (Source: Wikipedia, Ultimate Kho Kho.)
