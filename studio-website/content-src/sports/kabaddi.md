## What is kabaddi?

Kabaddi is a team contact sport from South Asia. Two teams of seven take turns to attack and defend on a small court split in half by a midline.

The attacker is called the **raider**. One raider at a time crosses into the other half, tries to touch as many defenders as possible, and then has to get back over the midline. The defenders try to stop that by tackling and holding the raider.

It needs no ball and no equipment, just a flat court, so it has long been played in villages and school yards. Today it is also a fast, televised professional sport, led by India's Pro Kabaddi League.

## History and origins

### Ancient roots and tradition

Kabaddi almost certainly began on the Indian subcontinent, but nobody can date it precisely. Wikipedia's article, citing historians such as Ronojoy Sen, says it probably goes back thousands of years, possibly to the Vedic period.

Some stories are tradition rather than proven history. A popular one compares kabaddi to the *Mahabharata* episode in which the young warrior Abhimanyu enters the Kauravas' *chakravyuha* (a circular battle formation) and is trapped inside it, much like a raider surrounded by defenders. This is a comparison, not evidence that the epic describes kabaddi. Tales that Krishna or the Buddha played an early form of the game are also traditions. Other accounts place the game's origin in ancient Tamilakam (South India) or in Persia.

Even the name is debated. An often-quoted explanation links "kabaddi" to Tamil words meaning "holding hands", but scholars such as the anthropologist Joseph Alter say the word's origin is unclear.

### Many local names

The same basic game has many regional names, for example:

- *hu-tu-tu* in western India
- *chedugudu* in Andhra Pradesh
- *ha-du-du* (hadudu) in Bengal and Bangladesh
- *sadugudu* in Tamil Nadu
- *gudu* in Sri Lanka and *bhavatik* in the Maldives

### Traditional forms

Before the modern rules, many local versions existed. The best known today is **circle style**, also called **Punjabi kabaddi**. It is played on a round pitch about 22 m across, with one raider against one defender at a time, and players are not sent off the field.

Other older Indian forms include *Sanjeevani*, where a player who is out can be revived, and *Gaminee*, where out players stay out until the whole side is out. Beach kabaddi and indoor versions with smaller teams are also played.

### The modern game

Modern rules for the rectangular "standard" game were written in Maharashtra in 1921, revised in 1923, and used at a national tournament in Baroda that year. Kabaddi was shown at the 1936 Berlin Olympics as a demonstration sport.

National organisation followed: the All India Kabaddi Federation was set up in 1950, and the **Amateur Kabaddi Federation of India (AKFI)** in 1972. Kabaddi also became Bangladesh's national sport in 1972. The first Asian Kabaddi Championship was held in 1980, and kabaddi became an Asian Games medal sport in 1990.

## How it's played

These are the standard-style rules used in international events such as the Asian Games. Professional leagues add their own twists, and details change between editions and seasons.

### The court

{{diagram}}

The court is a rectangle, about **13 m by 10 m** for men and **12 m by 8 m** for women (smaller for juniors). A **midline** divides it into two halves.

In each half there are two more lines parallel to the midline:

- The **baulk line**, 3.75 m from the midline for men (3 m for women). A raider must cross it for the raid to count.
- The **bonus line**, 1 m beyond the baulk line, used for bonus points.

Along the two long sides are 1 m strips called the **lobbies**. Around the whole court there is a clear safety area of about 4 m.

### Teams and positions

Each team has seven players on court and a few substitutes. Players are usually described as:

- **Raiders**, who attack.
- **Defenders**, often split into **corners** (at the two ends of the defensive line) and **covers** (in the middle).
- **All-rounders**, who both raid and defend.

### The raid

Teams take turns sending in one raider. The raider must cross the midline and the baulk line, try to touch defenders with a hand or foot, and return to their own half.

Traditionally there was no clock: the raid lasted only as long as one breath. To prove they were not breathing in, raiders keep chanting "kabaddi, kabaddi..." without pause. This chant is called the **cant**. Stopping the cant in the opponents' half is a technical foul.

Modern rules add a **30-second limit** on each raid. This came in with the Pro Kabaddi League in 2014, and the 30-second clock is now the main time limit in the standard game. Rule summaries still describe the cant as well, so the two exist side by side.

### Scoring

- **Touch points:** the raider scores one point for every defender touched, if the raider gets back to their own half. Those defenders are out.
- **Tackle point:** if the defenders stop the raider getting home, the defending side scores a point and the raider is out.
- **Bonus point:** if six or more defenders are on court, a raider can earn a bonus point by reaching the bonus line with one foot while the other foot is in the air.
- **All out:** if a team puts all seven opponents out, it scores **two extra points** and the whole opposing team comes back on.
- **Revival:** when a team scores a point, one of its out players comes back, in the order they went out. Bonus points and technical points do not revive players.

A player who steps out of bounds during a raid is out. A raider who fails to cross the baulk line without touching anyone is also out.

### Professional league extras

The Pro Kabaddi League uses extra rules and terms:

- **Do-or-die raid:** after two empty raids in a row, the team's next raider must score or is out.
- **Super raid:** a raid worth three or more points.
- **Super tackle:** a tackle by a team with three or fewer defenders left on court; it earns an extra point.

The lobby rules have also changed over time. ESPN reported that from Season 9 (2022) a defender who follows a raider into the lobby is no longer ruled out.

### Fouls and cards

Misconduct is dealt with by cards:

- A **green card** is a warning.
- A **yellow card** suspends a player for two minutes.
- A **red card** sends a player off, possibly for the rest of the tournament.

### Officials

A standard match has a **referee**, **two umpires**, a **scorer** and two assistant scorers. The referee handles the coin toss, the time and substitutions; the umpires watch the lines and contacts.

### Match length

A match has **two halves**, each **20 minutes for men** and **15 minutes for women**, with a five-minute break. Teams change ends at half time.

If a match that needs a winner ends level, each team makes **five raids** with five different raiders. If it is still tied, a coin toss decides who takes a **golden raid**, and the first team to score wins.

## Equipment

Kabaddi needs very little:

- A flat, soft court. Top-level games use synthetic mats; village games are often on earth.
- Shorts or a sports kit with numbered shirts.
- Light, grippy shoes, or bare feet in traditional matches.

There is no ball, stick or protective gear.


## Where it's played today

### Governing bodies

- The **International Kabaddi Federation (IKF)** was formed in 2004 as the first worldwide governing body.
- The **Asian Kabaddi Federation** runs the sport in Asia and is linked to the Olympic Council of Asia.
- The **Amateur Kabaddi Federation of India (AKFI)**, formed in 1972, runs the sport in India.
- **World Kabaddi**, founded in 2018, runs a separate international circuit. Circle-style events are organised by other bodies.

### Asian Games

Kabaddi was a demonstration event in 1951 and 1982 and became a medal sport in **1990**. India won the men's gold at every Games from 1990 to 2014.

In 2018, Iran won both the men's and women's titles. India took both golds back at the 2022 Games in Hangzhou (held in 2023), beating Iran 33–29 in the men's final. At the 2026 Games in Aichi–Nagoya, India beat Iran in both finals again: 40–34 for the men and 37–34 for the women.

### World Cups

The IKF's standard-style **Kabaddi World Cup** has been held in 2004 (Mumbai), 2007 (Panvel) and 2016 (Ahmedabad). India won all three, beating Iran in each final. Women's editions were held in 2012 (Patna) and 2025 (Dhaka), both won by India.

A separate **circle-style Kabaddi World Cup** was mostly held in Punjab, India, from 2010. India won every men's edition until 2020, when Pakistan won at home.

### Pro Kabaddi League

The **Pro Kabaddi League (PKL)** began in 2014 with eight teams and grew to 12. It is shown on Star Sports. Jaipur Pink Panthers won the first season, beating U Mumba 35–24.

Patna Pirates have won the most titles, three in a row (two seasons in 2016, then 2017). Dabang Delhi K.C. won the 2025 season, beating Puneri Paltan 31–28 in the final.

Other leagues have followed, such as Pakistan's one-off Super Kabaddi League (2018) and the Nepal Kabaddi League (first held in 2025). Europe has had its own championship since 2019.

## Famous moments and players

- **Pardeep Narwal's 34 points (2017).** In a Season 5 eliminator in Mumbai on 23 October 2017, Narwal scored 34 raid points as Patna Pirates beat Haryana Steelers 69–30, then a league record. The official report also notes one super raid that put out six Haryana players. Nicknamed the "Dubki King", he won three straight PKL titles with Patna and retired in 2025. (Source: Pro Kabaddi; Wikipedia.)
- **Pawan Sehrawat's 39 points (2019).** In Season 7, Sehrawat scored 39 raid points for Bengaluru Bulls in a 59–36 win over Haryana Steelers, breaking Narwal's record and outscoring the whole opposing team. (Source: Pro Kabaddi.)
- **Anup Kumar.** Known as "Captain Cool", he won Asian Games gold in 2010 and 2014, captained India to the 2016 World Cup title and led U Mumba to the 2015 PKL title. He received the Arjuna Award in 2012 and retired in 2018. (Source: Wikipedia.)
- **Iran's 2018 double.** Iran ended India's long winning run at the Asian Games by taking both the men's and women's gold medals in Jakarta–Palembang. (Source: Wikipedia, Kabaddi at the Asian Games.)
