## What is floorball?

Floorball is an indoor team sport played with light sticks and a hollow plastic ball. It looks a little like ice hockey, but it is played in shoes on a hard floor. Body checking is not allowed, and the stick must stay low.

Each team has **five field players and a goalkeeper** on the rink. The goalkeeper plays without a stick. The aim is simple: score more goals than the other team.

The game is fast and cheap to start. All you need is a stick, a ball, a gym and some goals. That has helped it spread from schools and clubs to a professional top level in northern Europe.

The sport has different names in different countries. Swedes call it *innebandy* ("indoor bandy") and Finns call it *salibandy* ("hall bandy"). In Switzerland it is *unihockey*.

## History and origins

### Gym-hall roots

People have played stick-and-ball games indoors for a long time. Plastic floor-hockey sticks and balls were used in many US school gyms in the 1950s and 1960s.

Floorball as we know it took shape in **Sweden** from the late 1960s and through the 1970s. Sources disagree on exact dates and on which club came first.

Wikipedia says basic rules were set in Sweden in **1979**, and official match rules were first written down in **1981**. Sweden's national association joined the IFF's list of members with a founding date of 7 November 1981.

### An international federation

The **International Floorball Federation (IFF)** was founded on **12 April 1986** in Huskvarna, Sweden. Its three founders were the national associations of **Sweden, Finland and Switzerland**. The IFF is now based in Helsinki. As of 2024 it had 81 member associations.

The IFF's first big event was the European Cup in 1993. That club competition is now called the **Champions Cup**.

- **Men's World Championships:** first held in **1996** in Stockholm. Sweden beat Finland 5–0 in the final.
- **Women's World Championships:** first held in **1997** in Mariehamn, Finland. Sweden beat Finland 4–2 in the final.

The International Olympic Committee recognised the IFF in December 2008. In July 2011 it welcomed the IFF as a Recognised International Sports Federation. Floorball is not an Olympic sport.

## How it's played

The rules here follow the **IFF Rules of the Game, 2026 edition**, in force from **1 July 2026**. The IFF published this edition in February 2026, and it replaced the 2022 edition. Most of the basics below are the same in both editions.

### The rink

{{diagram}}

- **Size:** a standard rink is **40 m × 20 m** (smallest allowed: 36 m × 18 m). It is surrounded by IFF-approved **boards** with rounded corners, about 50 cm high.
- **Centre line and centre spot:** split the rink into two halves. Each period starts with a face-off at the centre spot.
- **Goal crease:** a **4 m × 5 m** rectangle in front of each goal, marked 2.85 m from the end boards. This is the goalkeeper's working area.
- **Goalkeeper area:** a small **1 m × 2.5 m** rectangle inside the crease. Field players may not enter it.
- **Goal line:** the back line of the goalkeeper area. Goal posts stand on it, **1.6 m** apart.
- **Goals:** IFF-approved goal cages, **160 cm wide and 115 cm high**.
- **Face-off dots:** on the centre line, and in the corners on the goal-line extensions, 1.5 m from the side boards.

### Teams and substitutions

A team may list up to **20 players**. At most **six** may be on the rink at once: five field players plus a goalkeeper, or six field players with no goalkeeper.

Substitutions are **unlimited and on the fly**, through the team's own substitution zone. Players usually play short, intense shifts.

### Positions

The rules only name two roles: **goalkeepers** and **field players**. Teams usually have two or three lines of five field players, and they change whole lines at a time.

**Goalkeepers** stop shots with their hands and body. Inside the crease they may catch the ball and throw it. If the throw goes over the centre line, it must first touch the floor, a board or a player. They may hold the ball for no more than **3 seconds**.

Goalkeepers may not take a deliberate pass from a team-mate with their hands. If a goalkeeper leaves the crease completely, they count as a field player without a stick.

### Scoring

A **goal** is scored when the whole ball crosses the goal line from the front, played correctly. You may not kick the ball in on purpose or direct it in with your body.

A face-off, hit-in or free-hit can go straight into the goal.

### Key rules on the stick and body

- **Ball below the knee:** You may not play, or try to play, the ball **above knee level** with your stick, foot or lower leg. Stopping the ball with your thigh is allowed.
- **No high sticks:** Your blade may not go **above waist level** in your back-swing or follow-through when other players are near.
- **No hitting the stick:** You may not hit, block, lift or kick an opponent's stick.
- **No checking:** Only **shoulder-to-shoulder** contact is allowed. You may not push, hold or trip opponents, or back into them.
- **No sticks between the legs:** You may not put your stick, foot or leg between an opponent's legs or feet.
- **Stay on your feet:** You may not jump to stop the ball, or play it with your head.
- **No hands:** Field players may not play the ball with a hand or arm. Playing it while lying on the floor to gain an advantage is also a foul.

### Free-hits and other restarts

Most fouls lead to a **free-hit** for the other team, taken where the foul happened. Opponents must stand at least **3 m** away, sticks included.

The ball must be **hit**, not dragged or flicked. A free-hit is never taken closer than 3.5 m to the goalkeeper area. If the fouled team still has the ball, the referees play **advantage**.

When the ball goes over the boards, the other team takes a **hit-in**, 1.5 m from the boards. A **face-off** restarts play when no team can be given the ball.

### Penalties

Serious fouls send a player to the **penalty bench**, so the team plays short-handed. The 2022 rules changed the old system: the **5-minute penalty was replaced by a "2+2"** major penalty. The 2026 rules keep this structure.

- **Minor bench penalty: 2 minutes.** For example, for slashing at an opponent's stick to win a big advantage, playing the ball above the waist, careless stick or body play, a hand ball, too many players on the rink, or protesting.
- **Major bench penalty: 2 + 2 minutes.** For reckless play with the stick, hooking an opponent's body with the stick, throwing a stick at the ball, or reckless physical play.
- **10-minute personal penalty:** For unsporting behaviour or diving. The player sits out, but the team can replace them on the rink. It always comes with a bench penalty.
- **Technical match penalty:** Sends a player off for the rest of the match, for technical offences such as using a non-approved stick.
- **Match penalty:** Sends a player off for the rest of the match and the next match, and may lead to more punishment. It covers violent or brutal play, fights and gross misconduct.

If the opponents score during a bench penalty, the penalty usually ends early.

### Penalty shots

A **penalty shot** is given when a foul stops a clear goal-scoring situation. The shooter starts with the stick from a spot 5 m from the centre line, midway across the rink, and goes one-on-one with the goalkeeper. Every other player waits in the substitution zone.

### Officials

A match is led by **two referees with equal authority**. A neutral **match secretariat** keeps the match record, runs the clock and makes announcements.

### Match length

- **Regular time:** **3 × 20 minutes** of effective time (the clock stops at every whistle), with two **10-minute** breaks. Organisers may allow shorter games, but not shorter than 2 × 15 minutes.
- **Time out:** each team gets one, lasting **30 seconds**.
- **Overtime:** if a match must have a winner, there is **10 minutes of sudden-death overtime**, then **penalty shots**, five per team to start.

## Equipment

- **Stick:** a shaft, usually carbon fibre, with a plastic blade. It must be IFF-approved and marked. The blade may not be sharp, and its hook may not exceed **30 mm**. Wikipedia gives the usual limits as at most **114 cm** long and **350 g**.
- **Ball:** IFF-approved hollow plastic, single-coloured, **72 mm** across, about **23 g**, with **26 holes**. Shots have been recorded at around 200 km/h.
- **Goalkeeper:** must wear an IFF-approved **face mask**, a jersey and **long trousers**. Padding must fit the body and must not be shaped to cover the goal.
- **Players:** jersey, shorts, socks and **indoor shoes**. Jewellery is banned. The IFF advises national associations to make protective **goggles** compulsory for under-17s.

## Where it's played today

The **IFF** governs floorball worldwide. National associations run their own leagues and cups, such as Czechia's Superliga (pictured below).

The four leading nations are **Sweden, Finland, Switzerland and Czechia**. Sweden and Finland have won every men's world title.

Major competitions:

- **Men's World Floorball Championships:** every two years, in even-numbered years. Sweden has **10** titles and Finland **5**. The 2026 edition is in **Tampere, Finland**.
- **Women's World Floorball Championships:** every two years, in odd-numbered years. Sweden has **11** titles, Finland **2** and Switzerland **2**.
- **U19 World Championships** for young players.
- **Champions Cup:** the top club tournament for the best men's and women's clubs. It began as the European Cup in 1993.
- **The World Games:** floorball has been played at the multi-sport World Games, most recently in Chengdu, China, in 2025.

## Famous moments and players

- **Finland end Sweden's run (2008):** In the men's world final in Prague, Finland beat Sweden **7–6 in overtime**. It was Finland's first world title. It ended Sweden's six straight titles and was the first world championship game Sweden had ever lost. Source: [2008 Men's World Floorball Championships - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Men%27s_World_Floorball_Championships).
- **Overtime in Malmö (2024):** Finland beat hosts Sweden **5–4 in overtime** in the men's world final. Source: [Men's World Floorball Championship - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Men%27s_World_Floorball_Championship).
- **Switzerland's women (2025):** In Ostrava, Switzerland beat the Czech Republic **2–0** to win their second world title. A record 8,844 spectators watched the final. Source: [2025 Women's World Floorball Championships - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Women%27s_World_Floorball_Championships).
- **Mika Kohonen (Finland):** Played in 11 men's World Championships from 1998 to 2018 and won a medal at every one, including four golds. He was voted the world's best player a record five times in the Innebandymagazinet poll. He entered the IFF Hall of Fame in 2021. Source: [Mika Kohonen - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mika_Kohonen).
