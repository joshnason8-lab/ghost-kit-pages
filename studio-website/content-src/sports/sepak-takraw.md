## What is sepak takraw?

Sepak takraw is often called "kick volleyball". Two teams face each other across a net on a court the size of a badminton doubles court. They keep a light woven ball in the air and send it over the net using their feet, knees, chest and head, but never their hands or arms.

Each side may touch the ball up to three times before it must go over. A point is won when the ball lands in the opponent's court or the other team makes a fault.

The headline move is the spike. The attacker leaps, flips backwards or sideways and kicks the ball down over the net with the foot higher than the head. It is one of the most acrobatic sights in any sport.

The sport is hugely popular in Southeast Asia, above all in Thailand and Malaysia, and is now played in dozens of countries.

## History and origins

### An old game of keeping the ball up

Long before there was a net, people across Southeast Asia played circle games of keeping a woven rattan ball in the air without using their hands. The game had many local names:

- **Sepak raga** in Malaysia, Singapore, Brunei and Indonesia.
- **Takraw** in Thailand, with a related game in Laos.
- **Sipa** in the Philippines, a traditional game that is related to sepak takraw and has its own over-the-net forms.
- **Chinlone** in Myanmar. This is a separate, mostly non-competitive game: a team of about six players passes the ball around a circle, judged on skill and style rather than beating an opponent.

How old these games are is hard to pin down. The Malay Annals describe a rattan-ball game in the 15th-century Malacca Sultanate, and Thai records place it in the reign of King Naresuan (1590–1605). Kicking games such as Chinese cuju are sometimes named as distant relatives. Treat exact origins as tradition rather than settled history.

### Adding a net

The net version is modern and Malayan. According to the history in the Wikipedia article on the sport, sepak raga was played over a net on a badminton court in Seremban in 1935. In 1945, Hamid Mydin presented a net game called "sepak raga jaring" in Penang, and the first organised competition followed there that year.

### One name, one set of rules

In 1960, officials agreed early rules in Kuala Lumpur and set up a Malayan sepak raga federation. Representatives from Malaya, Singapore, Myanmar and Thailand also met in Kuala Lumpur and settled on a compromise name. **"Sepak"** is Malay for "kick", and **"takraw"** comes from Thai and refers to a woven rattan ball. Together the name means roughly "kick the woven ball".

The Asian Sepaktakraw Federation (ASTAF) followed in 1965, and the sport was a medal event at that year's Southeast Asian Peninsular Games in Kuala Lumpur. Thailand began replacing rattan balls with woven synthetic ones in 1982.

### Going international

The **International Sepaktakraw Federation (ISTAF)** was formed in 1988 by Malaysia, Thailand, Singapore, Indonesia and Myanmar. Sepak takraw became a medal sport at the Asian Games in Beijing in 1990, and women's events were added in 1998.

ISTAF launched its World Cup and its SuperSeries circuit in 2011.

## How it's played

These rules follow ISTAF's **Law of the Game 2024**, which has been in force since 1 February 2024. Older guides, and some local events, still use the previous 21-point scoring, explained below.

### The court

The court is **13.4 m long and 6.1 m wide**, with no obstacles up to 8 m above the floor and a free zone at least 3 m wide all around. A centre line divides the court into two halves, one per team.

At each end of the centre line, a **quarter circle** with a 0.9 m radius is marked where it meets each side line. In each half there is a **service circle** with a 0.3 m radius, centred 2.45 m from the back line and 3.05 m from each side line.

{{diagram}}

### The net

The net is 0.7 m deep and stretches across the centre line. Its top is **1.52 m high in the middle for men and 1.42 m for women**, rising to 1.55 m and 1.45 m at the posts. That is about badminton height, low enough to make the overhead kicks possible.

### Teams and positions

The classic format is the **regu**: three players a side, plus up to two reserves.

- **Tekong (server)**: plays at the back and serves from the service circle.
- **Left inside**: often called the feeder or setter, who sets the ball up for an attack.
- **Right inside**: often called the striker or killer, who spikes the ball over the net.

The two inside players stand in the quarter circles while their team serves. In a **team event**, each country fields three different regus, and the team that wins two of the three regu matches wins.

ISTAF also has **doubles** (two players a side) and **quadrant** (four a side) formats, with team versions of each. In those formats the server stands behind the back line rather than in the service circle.


### Serving

The serving side's inside player throws the ball to the tekong, who must kick it straight over the net. The tekong's other foot must stay in the service circle until the kick. Since 2024, **serve alternates after every point**, whoever wins it.

### Scoring

Every fault gives a point to the other side, whether that side was serving or not (rally scoring).

- A set is won by the first side to **15 points**.
- At **14–14**, the referee announces "setting up to 17", and the set goes to the first side to reach 17.
- A match is **best of three sets**, with two-minute breaks between sets. Teams change ends at 8 points in the third set.

**Which version?** These are the ISTAF rules since 1 February 2024. Before that, for about a decade, ISTAF sets went to 21 points, capped at 25 after 20–20, and each side served three times in a row. ISTAF changed it to shorten matches. Some national associations, including Malaysia's, argued for a return to 21 points, but in 2024 ISTAF said the 15-point system would stay.

### Touches and faults

Each side may play the ball **up to three times** in succession before it crosses the net. Common faults include:

- Touching the ball with the hand or arm.
- Playing the ball more than three times.
- Catching or trapping the ball under the arm, between the legs or against the body.
- Touching the net, the posts or the referee's chair with any part of the body or kit.
- Crossing into the other court, except in the natural follow-through of a kick or block.
- Touching the ball on the opponent's side.
- The ball hitting the ceiling, a wall or any object other than the net.
- On the serve: inside players moving out of their quarter circles, or the tekong lifting the planted foot before the kick.

### The officials

Under the 2024 Law of the Game, each match has a **Referee** at one end of the net, an **Assistant Referee** opposite, a **Court Referee** behind the Referee and **two Line Referees** along the side lines. An **Official Referee** supervises from off court.

Players and officials can be shown yellow and red cards. A player sent off may be replaced only if the team still has a substitution left in that set.

### Match length

There is no game clock: a match lasts until one side wins two sets. Each regu may call one time-out of up to a minute per set and make two substitutions per set.

## Equipment

- **The ball**: originally hand-woven rattan. Today's ISTAF ball is a single woven layer of synthetic fibre with 12 holes and 20 crossing points. ISTAF's 2024 rules give 0.41–0.43 m around and 170–180 g for men, and 0.42–0.44 m and 150–160 g for women. Rubber-covered balls are allowed only if ISTAF approves them.
- **The net and posts**: a fine nylon net with 6–8 cm mesh, held at 1.55 m (men) or 1.45 m (women) at the posts.
- **Kit**: a numbered jersey, shorts, socks and rubber-soled sports shoes without heels. The regu captain wears an armband on the left upper arm.

## Where it's played today

**ISTAF** governs the sport worldwide. Its secretariat is in Singapore, and its membership includes the Asian federation (ASTAF) and the European federation (FESTA). The sport's heartland is Southeast Asia, but there are national teams in South and East Asia, Europe, the Americas and beyond.

### Major competitions

- **Asian Games**: sepak takraw has been a medal sport since 1990.
- **Southeast Asian (SEA) Games**: a medal sport since the 1965 Southeast Asian Peninsular Games.
- **King's Cup**: the Takraw Association of Thailand's world championship, held since 1985 and often seen as the most prestigious event.
- **ISTAF World Cup**: first held in Kuala Lumpur in 2011.
- **ISTAF SuperSeries**: a circuit of tournaments that started in Bangkok in 2011.

Thailand is the dominant power. Malaysia is its great rival, and Myanmar, Vietnam, Indonesia, the Philippines, South Korea, Japan and India are all medal contenders.

## Famous moments and players

### Hamid Mydin and the first net game

Hamid Mydin is credited with presenting the net version of sepak raga in Penang in 1945, the step that turned a circle game into a competitive sport. ([Sepak takraw - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sepak_takraw))

### Thailand's Asian Games run

Malaysia topped the sepak takraw medal table at the first two Asian Games events, in 1990 and 1994. Thailand topped it at every Games from 1998 to 2022 and has won well over half of all the sport's Asian Games gold medals. ([Sepak takraw at the Asian Games - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sepak_takraw_at_the_Asian_Games))

### Chinlone at the SEA Games

When Myanmar hosted the 2013 SEA Games, chinlone, its traditional circle game, was contested for the first time. Its inclusion was controversial because other countries had little experience of it. ([Chinlone - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinlone))

### India's first World Cup gold

On 25 March 2025, at the Sepak Takraw World Cup in Patna, India's men's regu beat Japan 2–1 in the final (11–15, 15–11, 17–14). It was India's first ever World Cup gold. The final set was decided by the new "setting up to 17" rule. ([The Bridge](https://thebridge.in/others/sepak-takraw-world-cup-india-men-regu-team-win-gold-52646))
