## What is ulama?

Ulama is a team game in which players keep a heavy, solid rubber ball in play by striking it with their hips. It is the living descendant of the **Mesoamerican ballgame**, which people across what is now Mexico and Central America played for more than 3,000 years. It is often called one of the oldest continuously played sports in the world, and it is the oldest known sport played with a rubber ball.

The name comes from the Nahuatl (Aztec) word *ōllamaliztli*, "playing the ballgame", built on *ōlli*, rubber. The Classic Maya called the game *pitz*. In Spanish it is the *juego de pelota mesoamericano*, or *pelota maya* for Maya-region forms.

**"Pok-ta-pok"** is a modern popular name, not an ancient one. It goes back to a 1932 article by the archaeologist Frans Blom, who adapted it from the Yucatec Maya word *pokolpok*. Today it is mostly used for the revived "Maya ballgame" played in the Yucatán and Central America.

{{photo:p1}}

Ulama survives in three main forms, all from the state of Sinaloa in north-west Mexico:

- **Ulama de cadera** (hip ulama): the classic form, hit only with the hip. It is the closest to the ancient game and the most endangered.
- **Ulama de antebrazo** (forearm ulama): a lighter ball, about 0.5 kg, struck with a wrapped forearm on a smaller court, by teams of one to three. Women often play it.
- **Ulama de mazo** or *de palo* (mallet ulama): a small ball of about 500 g struck with a heavy two-handed wooden mallet, usually by teams of three or four.

## History and origins

### The earliest courts and balls

The oldest known ballcourt is at **Paso de la Amada**, on the Pacific coast of Chiapas, Mexico. When it was announced in 1998 it was dated to about 1400 BC in uncalibrated radiocarbon years. More recent reporting puts it at roughly 3,650 years old, which is about 1650 BCE.

The oldest balls are even earlier. At **El Manatí**, a waterlogged spring in Veracruz associated with the Olmec, archaeologists found a dozen rubber balls left as offerings, five of them dated to about 1700–1600 BC. In 2020 a court at **Etlatongo** in the Oaxaca highlands was dated to about 1374 BC. Where the game began is still an open question.

### Spread across Mesoamerica

By around 300 BC courts are found across Mesoamerica, and well over 1,300 ancient ballcourts are now known. Most are long, narrow alleys between sloping or vertical walls. Later courts closed off both ends, giving the classic **I-shape** seen from above. Some cities had many: El Tajín in Veracruz has around 17–20.

The famous **stone rings** high on the side walls are a late feature, not part of the original game. They appear in the Postclassic period among the Maya and continue with the Toltecs and Aztecs. Putting the ball through a ring was rare and seems to have ended the game; most games were probably decided on points.

### The Great Ballcourt at Chichén Itzá

The **Great Ballcourt** at Chichén Itzá in Yucatán is the largest in Mesoamerica. The whole complex is about 168 by 70 m. Its side walls are about 95 m long and 8 m high, with carved rings of feathered serpents near the top. Some scholars doubt such a giant court was used for ordinary play.

{{photo:p2}}

### Religion, politics and the question of sacrifice

The game was more than sport. It was tied to religion, cosmology and politics. Spanish-era accounts describe Aztec nobles betting heavily on games, and some researchers argue the game was used to settle disputes in place of war.

The best-known myth comes from the **Popol Vuh**, the K'iche' Maya book written down in the colonial period. In it, the Hero Twins Hunahpu and Xbalanque play ball against the lords of the underworld, Xibalba, who had earlier killed their father. The twins outwit the lords and rise to become the sun and moon. This is sacred story, not history, but it shows how closely the game was linked to death and rebirth.

Some ancient art links the game to human sacrifice. A relief panel at Chichén Itzá shows a ballplayer who has been beheaded, with blood shown as streams of snakes. Panels at El Tajín's South Ballcourt show a player about to be sacrificed.

What this does **not** show is settled. Explicit sacrifice imagery appears only from the Classic period onward and is not found everywhere. Scholars still debate how often sacrifice happened, who was killed (captives, skilled players or team captains), and whether it was the losers or the winners. The popular idea that every game ended in a death is not supported by the evidence. Many games were clearly played for recreation or betting.

### Conquest and survival

After the Spanish conquest in the 1520s, Spanish and church authorities suppressed the game, partly because of its religious meaning, and many courts were destroyed. In 1528 Hernán Cortés took a group of Indigenous people, including ballplayers, to the court of Emperor Charles V in Spain. The German artist **Christoph Weiditz** drew them there around 1528–29, and his drawings are among the earliest European pictures of the game.

{{photo:p3}}

The game survived in Sinaloa, far from the centres of colonial power. There, according to historian Manuel Aguilar-Moreno, Jesuit-led colonisation was less aggressive and the game was folded into Catholic festivals.

## How ulama de cadera is played

Rules vary between communities and there is no single written rulebook. The description below follows field research in Sinaloa by Aguilar-Moreno (2003–2013) and recent reporting.

### The court: the taste

Ulama is played on a temporary dirt court called the **taste**, from the Nahuatl *tlachtli*, ballcourt. It is long and very narrow, marked out with chalk or lines scratched in the ground.

{{diagram}}

- **Length:** roughly 60–70 m. Aguilar-Moreno gives about 60 m in one publication and about 225 ft (69 m) in another.
- **Width:** about 4 m (13 ft).
- **The analco:** the centre line that divides the court into two halves. In the village of Los Llanitos it is marked by two stones.
- **The chichis:** the end lines (also called *chivo*). Driving the ball past the other team's end line scores.

Treat these numbers as approximate: each community lays out its own taste.

### Teams and players

Players are called *taures*. Team sizes vary: Aguilar-Moreno reports three to five a side, other sources say five or more and sometimes up to twelve, and AP reports up to six. In hip ulama only the hip may touch the ball. Players drop into deep crouches to get a hip under a low ball.

### Serving

There are two serves: a high serve thrown over the analco (*male por arriba*) and a rolled serve along the ground (*male por abajo*). Which serve is used depends on the score.

### Scoring: rayas

Points are called **rayas** ("lines"), after the tally marks used to keep score. A team scores a raya when the other side:

- fails to return the ball past the analco, or
- lets the ball cross its own end line.

The first team to **eight rayas** wins. But the score is not simply added up. It swings up and down like a seesaw. A transitional phase called the **urria**, between 2 and 3 and between 6 and 7, decides whether a score goes up or wipes the other side's points back down. A team can lose all its points in one phase, and a tie is not allowed. That is why games can run for a very long time.

### Fouls

Fouls that give away a point include:

- touching the ball with any body part other than the hip
- hitting the ball out of turn, or missing it
- knocking it out of bounds
- letting it stop before it crosses the analco
- failing to announce a score

### The officials: veedores

Games are judged by **veedores**, usually older or retired players whose word is final. In matches between villages each side provides one. They step in only when the teams disagree about a play or a point.

### Match length

There is no clock. Because the score swings up and down, games tied to religious festivals could once last days, sometimes a whole week, according to AP. Today players usually agree a number of hours in advance.

## Equipment

### The ball

The hip ball is solid rubber and very heavy: sources give between about 3.2 kg and 4 kg (roughly 7–9 lb). Spanish chroniclers wrote that some players in Aztec times died after being struck by the ball.

Making a ball is a rare skill. Traditionally, latex is mixed with the juice of a plant root (*machacuana* in Sinaloa). Good latex is now so scarce that a single ball can cost around US$1,000.

### How ancient rubber was made

In 1999, **Dorothy Hosler, Sandra Burkett and Michael Tarkanian** at MIT showed in *Science* that Mesoamericans were making rubber by about 1600 BC. They mixed latex from the *Castilla elastica* tree with juice from a morning glory vine, *Ipomoea alba*. The team tested samples from two El Manatí balls and latex gathered in Chiapas, where rubber is still processed by traditional methods.

### Hip protection: the fajado

Players wear a set of leather and cloth gear called the **fajado**:

- **gamuza:** a loincloth of deer, cow or goat hide
- **chimali:** a leather belt about 5 cm wide around the waist and buttocks
- **faja:** a cloth band that supports the stomach
- **bota:** an extra leather pad some players wear over the hitting hip

### Ancient yokes, hachas and palmas

Ancient ballplayers wore padded belts called **yokes**, plus gloves, kneepads and headdresses. Museums hold many U-shaped **stone yokes**, flat carved **hachas** and tall **palmas** from the Gulf Coast. These stone pieces are too heavy to play in. Archaeologists see them as ceremonial copies of the real gear, used in ritual or burials.

## Where it's played today

### Sinaloa

Hip ulama is played in a handful of villages near Mazatlán, in southern Sinaloa: Los Llanitos, La Sávila, El Chamizal and La Mora Escarbada, plus a family in Escuinapa. A 2003 survey found it in just four communities, with only about 30–40 active players. Forearm and mallet ulama are stronger in northern Sinaloa, around Guamúchil and Mocorito.

Families have kept the game alive. Mexico News Daily reports that the Lizárraga family has played since around 1900 and has trained about 150 players.

### Revival

Interest grew after an ulama exhibition on the opening day of the **1968 Mexico City Olympics**. In the 1990s the Xcaret park on the Caribbean coast hired Sinaloan players to perform the game for visitors, which brought income but also a staged, tourist version of the game.

Today ulama is played and taught well beyond Sinaloa. Games are held regularly in Mexico City, at a cultural centre in Azcapotzalco, and AP estimated about 1,000 players in 2026, mainly in Mexico and Guatemala. Organisations named in reporting include **AJUPEME** (Asociación de Juego de Pelota Mesoamericano), which runs hip ulama in Mexico and the United States, and the Lizárraga-led **FEMUC Ulama de cadera**. In Belize the game is run by the **Belize Hipball Association**.

### Pelota maya and the World Cup

In the Yucatán and Central America a revived "Maya ballgame", often called **pok-ta-pok**, has its own tournaments. It is a modern reconstruction rather than an unbroken tradition, and its rules differ from ulama: a hoop may hang over the court and earn bonus points. A **Pok-ta-pok World Cup** began at Chichén Itzá in 2015, with later editions in Guatemala, El Salvador, Belize and Mexico. Teams have come from Mexico, Belize, Guatemala, Honduras and Panama, among others.

## Famous moments and players

- **1528–29, Weiditz's drawings.** Players brought to Spain by Cortés were drawn by Christoph Weiditz, in an album now in the Germanisches Nationalmuseum in Nuremberg ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christoph_Weiditz)).
- **1968, the Olympic exhibition.** Ulama was shown on the opening day of the Mexico City Olympics, sparking efforts to study and save it ([AP](https://jamaica-gleaner.com/article/news/20260511/mexico-how-3400-year-old-ballgame-survives-against-all-odds)).
- **1998, the oldest court.** Archaeologists announced the earthen court at Paso de la Amada, Chiapas ([Mesoamerican ballgame, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerican_ballgame)).
- **1999, the rubber recipe.** Hosler, Burkett and Tarkanian showed how ancient Mesoamericans made bouncy rubber ([MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/1999/rubber-0714)).
- **2003–2013, Proyecto Ulama.** Researchers from California State University, Los Angeles, documented the game in Sinaloa. They also made experimental balls and in 2013 taught young people in La Sávila to make them ([Aguilar-Moreno, 2015](https://www.elsevier.es/es-revista-anales-antropologia-95-articulo-ulama-pasado-presente-futuro-del-S0185122515716450)).
- **2004, Barcelona.** Players from Los Llanitos gave an exhibition at an international cultural festival in Barcelona ([Aguilar-Moreno, 2015](https://www.elsevier.es/es-revista-anales-antropologia-95-articulo-ulama-pasado-presente-futuro-del-S0185122515716450)).
- **2017–2021, Ek' Balam of Belize.** This team won the pok-ta-pok World Cup in 2017, 2019 and 2021, beating Mexico 34–10 in the 2021 final ([Mexico News Daily](https://mexiconewsdaily.com/mexico-living/pelota-maya-indigenous-pride-artful-gameplay)).
