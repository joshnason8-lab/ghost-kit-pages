## What is netball?

Netball is a team ball game for two sides of seven players. You score by throwing the ball through a ring on a tall post, a bit like basketball, but there is no backboard, no dribbling and no running with the ball.

Once you catch the ball you have three seconds to pass or shoot, and you can barely move your feet. Every player also has a fixed position that limits where on the court they may go. That makes netball a game of quick, precise passing and clever movement off the ball.

It is one of the most popular team sports for women and girls in the Commonwealth. Commonly cited figures put it at more than 20 million players in over 80 countries, and men's and mixed netball are growing too.

## History and origins

### From women's basketball

Netball grew out of basketball, which James Naismith invented in Springfield, Massachusetts, in 1891. Women began playing the new game almost at once, and teachers soon adapted it for girls and women.

Two adaptations matter for netball. In New Orleans, the teacher Clara Baer asked Naismith for the rules. She is said to have misread lines on his court diagram as zones that players could not leave. Her women's rules, published in 1895, kept players in areas of the court, and World Netball's history says this misreading was written into women's basketball rules by 1899.

In England, Martina Bergman-Österberg introduced a form of basketball to her students in Hampstead, London, in 1893. Her college later moved to Dartford in Kent. According to the Wikipedia history of the sport, the game was moved outdoors there in 1897, played with netted rings, and given the name "net ball".

The two strands are often told together, and sources differ on the exact dates and on how much each teacher shaped the game. What is clear is that zones, a ban on running with the ball and a goal without a backboard were in place very early.

### Rules, nations and a world body

The Ling Association in England published the first codified netball rules in 1901 (some accounts say 1900). The game spread through schools across the British Empire, but for decades each country played slightly differently, with seven, nine or even five players a side.

National bodies followed: New Zealand in 1924, the All England Net Ball Association in 1926 and an Australian association in 1927. Australia beat New Zealand 40–11 in the first international match, in Melbourne in 1938.

After an Australian tour of England in the 1950s showed how different the national rules were, representatives from England, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and the West Indies met in Sri Lanka in 1960. They agreed one set of seven-a-side rules and founded the International Federation of Women's Basketball and Netball.

That body was later renamed the International Netball Federation, and on 3 June 2021 it rebranded as **World Netball**.

### World Cups and the Commonwealth Games

The first world tournament was held in Eastbourne, England, in 1963, and it has been held about every four years since. Today it is called the Netball World Cup.

The International Olympic Committee recognised netball in 1995. Netball became a medal sport at the Commonwealth Games in Kuala Lumpur in 1998 and has been on the programme ever since. It has never been an Olympic sport.

Netball keeps evolving. World Netball developed a shorter, faster format, first called Fast Net and now FAST5, and in 2022 men played in a World Netball event for the first time, at the men's FAST5 World Series.

## How it's played

These rules follow the **Rules of Netball, 2024 Edition** from World Netball, the current edition. It came into force for international matches on 1 January 2024. National leagues can add their own variations.

### The court

The court is a rectangle **30.5 m long and 15.25 m wide** (100 by 50 feet). Two lines across it, called transverse lines, divide it into three equal parts: a goal third at each end and a centre third in the middle.

In the middle of the court is a small **centre circle, 0.9 m (3 ft) across**. At each end is a **goal circle**, a semicircle with a radius of 4.9 m (16 ft) measured from the middle of the goal line.

The goalpost stands at the middle of each goal line. It is 3.05 m (10 ft) high, with a ring of 380 mm (15 in) inside diameter and a net open at the bottom. There is no backboard.

### Seven players and their areas

Each team has seven players on court. Each wears a bib with position initials, and each may only play in certain areas:

- **Goal Shooter (GS)**: the attacking goal third, including the goal circle.
- **Goal Attack (GA)**: the centre third and the attacking goal third, including the goal circle.
- **Wing Attack (WA)**: the centre third and the attacking goal third, but not the goal circle.
- **Centre (C)**: everywhere except the two goal circles.
- **Wing Defence (WD)**: the centre third and the defending goal third, but not the goal circle.
- **Goal Defence (GD)**: the centre third and the defending goal third, including the goal circle.
- **Goal Keeper (GK)**: the defending goal third, including the goal circle.

Going into an area your position does not allow is called **offside**. The other team gets a free pass.

### Scoring

Only the Goal Shooter and Goal Attack can score, and only from **inside the goal circle**. A goal counts one point, and the team with more goals at the end wins.

Some professional leagues add a two-point **Super Shot**. This is a competition rule, not part of World Netball's rules. Suncorp Super Netball in Australia introduced it in 2020: in the last five minutes of each quarter, a goal from a marked zone at the outer edge of the circle counts two. The ANZ Premiership in New Zealand and the Netball Super League in Britain added their own versions in 2025.

### The centre pass

Play starts with a **centre pass**, taken by the Centre standing in the centre circle. It restarts the same way after every goal, and teams take turns no matter who scored.

When the umpire whistles, the other players may move into the centre third. The pass must be caught or touched by a player standing in, or landing in, the centre third.

### Key rules

- **Footwork**: after catching the ball, a player can pivot or step with the other foot, but must pass or shoot before the landing foot touches the ground again. No running, dragging the foot or hopping.
- **Three seconds**: the ball must be passed or shot within three seconds of catching it ("held ball").
- **Obstruction**: a defender may try to block or intercept, but must stay at least **0.9 m (3 ft)** away from the player with the ball. The distance is measured on the ground from the attacker's landing foot.
- **Contact**: no pushing, tripping, holding, knocking the ball out of someone's hands or otherwise interfering with an opponent by contact, whether deliberate or accidental.
- **Over a third**: the ball cannot be thrown over a whole third. It must be caught or touched by a player in each third it passes through.
- **Short pass**: there must be room for an opponent to intercept between passer and receiver.
- **Replaying**: a player who drops the ball or misses a shot cannot be the first to touch it again, unless it has bounced off the post or ring.

The 2024 edition also scrapped the old toss-up, which was used when two players held the ball at the same time.

### Fouls and penalties

Minor infringements such as footwork, held ball, offside and over a third give the other team a **free pass**. A player taking a free pass cannot shoot.

Major infringements, obstruction and contact, give a **penalty pass**. If it is awarded in the goal circle, the Goal Shooter or Goal Attack can choose to pass or shoot. Until the penalty is taken, the player who caused it must stand beside the thrower and take no part in play.

For repeated or dangerous play, umpires can warn a player, **suspend** them for two minutes of playing time, or order them off for the rest of the match.

### The officials

A match is controlled by **two umpires**, each in charge of one half of the court and one side line, with a reserve umpire on the bench. Scorers and timekeepers help them.

### Match length

A match is **four quarters of 15 minutes**. There are four-minute breaks after the first and third quarters and a 12-minute half-time (eight minutes if organisers and both teams agree). Teams change ends every quarter. Tournaments can use extra time to settle a tied match.

## Equipment

- **Ball**: round, made of leather, rubber or a similar synthetic material, 690–710 mm around and 400–450 g.
- **Goalposts**: padded metal poles 3.05 m high with a ring and net and no backboard.
- **Bibs**: position initials 150 mm high, worn front and back.
- **Clothing**: team uniform and sports shoes without spikes. Body piercings, including earrings, may not be worn.
- **Court**: preferably a sprung wooden floor, but netball is also played outdoors on hard courts and grass.

## Where it's played today

**World Netball**, based in the Manchester area of England, runs the international game. In April 2025 it had 63 full and 12 associate member nations across five regions: Africa, the Americas, Asia, Europe and Oceania.

The strongest nations come mostly from the Commonwealth: **Australia, New Zealand, England, Jamaica and South Africa**. Netball is also a major sport in Malawi, elsewhere in Africa and the Caribbean, and in Pacific island nations.

### Major competitions

- **Netball World Cup**: every four years. The 2027 tournament is planned for Sydney.
- **Commonwealth Games**: netball has been a medal sport since 1998.
- **Netball Quad Series**: a regular test series between the top nations.
- **FAST5 World Series**: the short, high-scoring format.

### Professional leagues

- **Suncorp Super Netball** in Australia.
- **ANZ Premiership** in New Zealand.
- **Netball Super League** in Britain (formerly the Superleague, started in 2005).

Many Jamaican, South African, Malawian and English internationals play in these leagues, especially in Australia.

## Famous moments and players

### Australia's World Cup record

Australia is the most successful team in netball history. By the Wikipedia count of Netball World Cup results, it has won 12 world titles, most recently beating England 61–45 in the 2023 final in Cape Town. ([Netball World Cup - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netball_World_Cup))

### New Zealand by one goal, 2019

At the 2019 Netball World Cup in Liverpool, the New Zealand Silver Ferns beat Australia 52–51 in the final. It was New Zealand's fifth world title, including one shared in 1979. ([Netball World Cup - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netball_World_Cup))

### England's last-second gold, 2018

In the Commonwealth Games final on the Gold Coast in April 2018, England trailed hosts Australia by up to four goals in the last quarter. Helen Housby then scored on the final buzzer to win 52–51. It was England's first Commonwealth Games netball gold. ([World Netball](https://netball.sport/gc2018-england-win-gold-in-thrilling-final/))

### Irene van Dyk

The goal shooter Irene van Dyk played 72 internationals for South Africa (1994–1999) and 145 for New Zealand (2000–2014). That made her the most-capped netball international in the world. She won the 2003 world title with New Zealand. ([Irene van Dyk - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irene_van_Dyk))
